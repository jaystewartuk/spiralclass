# D-196. Legal text is translated by a person, English controls, and a new language does not launch without its terms and privacy policy (2026-10-04)

**Status.** Decided, not built. Gates #178 step B2, and every new language
under step E.

**What changed.**

- **Who translates.** A professional legal translator: a person, commissioned
  by the operator. Never a machine, and never a general native reviewer
  standing in for one. D-81 and D-128 already forbid machine translation, and
  that stands.
- **Which language controls.** English, the language the terms are written in
  and the language of their governing law (England and Wales). Every
  translation opens with a clause, in its own language, saying three things:
  it is a translation; if it differs from the English, the English governs;
  and where the reader's own law gives them the right to rely on the
  translated text, that right is not taken away. The clause does not pretend
  to override consumer law.
- **Where it lives.** As data modules under `packages/shared/src/legal/`, the
  way `privacy-policy.ts` is today. Not in the per-locale catalog, where
  D-128 found a machine translation of legal text had already happened.
- **Launching a language.** A locale is not launched (offered in the picker,
  matched from the browser, listed in `hreflang`) until its terms and its
  privacy policy exist as person-made translations.
- **French is the one exception.** It launched under D-81 with legal pages in
  English. Its translation is the first to commission. Until it lands, the
  French legal pages say, in French, that the binding text is in English and
  that no French version exists yet.
- **A stale translation is not shown as current.** Each translation records
  the effective date of the English it translates. When the English changes,
  the page shows the new English, with a notice in the reader's language that
  the translation is being updated. The previous translation stays reachable,
  labelled as a translation of an earlier version. A test pins this. It does
  not fail the build, because an urgent change to the terms cannot wait on a
  translator.

**Why.** D-81 let French launch with English legal pages, and called that
acceptable "but worth revisiting before marketing French heavily". D-193 is
that moment: it makes the French pages findable by search for the first time.
A French reader who arrives from search should not meet terms they cannot read
at the point where they are asked to agree to them.

The existing Spanish terms show the gap from the other side:

- Two authored versions of `/terms` exist, Spanish and English.
- Neither says which one controls if they differ.
- Nothing records whether a person translated the Spanish one.

A language without a controlling clause has two binding texts that nobody
keeps in step.

**What was considered and rejected.**

- **Each translation controls in its own language.** The operator would carry
  N contracts in N languages, under English law, with no one able to read most
  of them. A mistranslation becomes binding.
- **Launch a language with English legal text, as French did.** Cheaper, and
  how French shipped. It puts a contract in front of a reader who cannot read
  it, which is the case consumer-language rules exist for.
- **Machine translation marked "for convenience only".** D-81 and D-128 rule
  it out. A convenience translation is still what the reader relies on.
- **Fail the build when a translation is stale.** It makes every urgent change
  to the terms wait on a translator. Showing the current English is honest and
  never blocks.

**What it constrains.**

- No legal text in the catalog, and no machine translation of it, ever.
- A new locale's launch (`launched: true`, #178 step E) needs both documents.
- `apps/web/tests/config/subprocessors.test.ts` holds the English original.
  A translation of the privacy policy renders from the same register, so the
  supplier list cannot drift by language.
- **The `/terms` Spanish version needs a controlling clause added.** When it
  moves into a data module (#178 step A5), it becomes a translation like any
  other, carrying the clause and the English version it translates.

**What was affected.** Nothing yet. Step B2 adds the translations and the
staleness rule. Step A5 moves `/terms` out of the page component.

**Trade-offs accepted, and unresolved risks.**

- **Money and time per language.** Each launch costs two legal translations,
  and each material change to the English costs an update to every one. The
  cost is the operator's to set; this record does not estimate it.
- **French readers see English legal text until the commission lands.** That
  is the gap D-81 accepted, now with a stated end.
- **Consumer-language law is not settled here.** Some countries require
  consumer terms in the local language, and some give the consumer's version
  priority whatever the clause says. France and Quebec are the cases usually
  cited. This reading comes from secondary summaries. A lawyer has to
  confirm it before it decides which languages are offered in which countries.
- **Who the contract is with is unchanged.** The teacher is merchant of record
  for lessons (D-143). These are the platform's own terms with teachers and
  students.
